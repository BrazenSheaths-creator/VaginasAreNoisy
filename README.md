Imagine if vaginal flatulence were a constant and ubiquitous phenomenon inherent to all fully-formed and mature vaginas, as inevitable as the propensity of unsupported male genitals to sway pendulously without any voluntary control due simply to physics acting on the structure. Suppose that vaginal flatulence is: \

— constant and loud, occurring tens to hundreds of times per hour depending on the individual and what she is doing, easily reaching 50 to 60 decibels. \

Assume the above statements are all well-established knowledge, not revelations. Further, assume that human anatomy and physiology are not different from reality, and nor are the laws of physics, instead adopting an alternative set of assumptions about how actual vaginal anatomy and physiology would interact with the actual laws of physics pertaining to gases and liquids. Importantly, imagine that this counterfactual framework explains existing reality such that nothing about the observable world changes except for the terminology we use, the medical services that exist (e.g. bime fitting), and cultural references to these realities in discourse and media. The only differences in reality should be in addition to existing characteristics of the world rather than alterations of them, except where totally unavoidable. It is a novel account of existing patterns, not a speculative rewrite of history or culture.

That said, some subtle differences would be inevitable, such as the existence of new semantic fields and terminology. Assume English would use the following terminology to describe this reality: \
— Bime: noun. A plug-like vaginal occlusive device worn so as to prevent the intake of external air and other fluids, preventing blating and lavage. Must be properly sized and shaped for individual vaginal caliber to be comfortable and effective, among a host of other vaginal measurements, prompting standardization in modernity (see Gynecometric) and fitting services as part of routine gynecological care. Usually made of medical grade silicone or similarly nonporous and hypoallergenic materials, and available through pharmacies, online retailers, and clinics via ordering from manufacturers. Etymology: Ultimately derived via Old English via Old French via Latin, from Ancient Greek βύσμα, meaning “plug, stopper.” \
— Blate: as a noun, a single instance of vaginal flatulence, or the act of experiencing it. As a verb, to experience vaginal flatulence, including the suction that precedes it. This has been the primary English term for the phenomenon since antiquity, though it lost respectability and was broadly replaced by ‘queef’ until it too lost respectability. Past tense is blet and past participle is blatten. Etymology: Ultimately derived via Old English via Proto-West-Germanic via Proto-Germanic, from Proto-Indo-European *bʰel, *bʰleh₁, semantically pertaining to blowing/growing/inflating. Usage: Considered more precise and explicit than “queef,” though not technical or formal (see Vaginal Flatulence). \
— Blatant: adjective. Subject to or characterized by vaginal flatulence, whether at all (e.g. prepubescent girls are not blatant, while pubescent girls and adult women are) or in degree or comparatively (e.g. "she's very blatant," “women generally get more blatant with age”). Also means flagrant or obvious, but this is derivative of the original meaning, as in something that stands out as conspicuously as a woman's blating does. \
— Flatulence: noun. Traditionally, any audible displacement of gas into or out of the body, but implicitly vaginal unless otherwise marked (e.g. digestive flatulence.) — Gynecometric: adjective. Of or pertaining to the measurement and assessment of female pelvic organs for the provision of bimes, pessaries, and other personal devices. The gynecometric classification system uses twelve-character alphanumeric gynecometric specs to standardize, identify, and distribute modern bimes, and was established by the International Organization for Standardization in 1941 and refined throughout the 20th century. Yields the noun gynecometrics and the concept of gynecometric specs, or simply “specs” in context. \

When analyzing how this vaginal flatulence reality relates to observable history and culture, distinguish carefully between three classes of phenomena: \
— Class A (Observable additions): Directly observable additions to reality that only exist because this vaginal framework is true. Examples: the terminology (blate, bime, tegnance), bime manufacturing and fitting services, gynecometric standardization, references to vaginal flatulence in media and discourse, tegnance/integnance as social practices pertaining to tegnance and the expression of vaginal flatulence. \
— Class B (Directly or Partly Attributable): Existing observable patterns in history, society, and culture that are shaped partly by reaction to or management of blating. A much larger category, but still a minority of all historical/cultural phenomena. Examples: restrictions on women's movement and exertion, posture norms and "ladylike" behavior standards, patterns of female domesticity, class-differentiated visibility of women's bodies through activity levels, certain aquatic and bathing practices pertaining to women. \
— Class C (Everything Else): The vastly larger category. All phenomena unaffected by blating except that people in this world who know about them also know about blating. Examples: geology, astrophysics, historical political powers, political revolutions, technological development, most economic systems, most art and literature (except where blating appears as content). \
— Critical principle: Statement 12 means that blating is one material reality that women historically accommodated. It is not the sole explanation for most aspects of history, nor for culture broadly. Blating is a factor among many, not a master cause. Avoid attributing phenomena to blating when they have independent, sufficient explanations, while also recognizing the immense explanatory power of vaginas-are-noisy for many aspects of our world that are otherwise difficult to understand or accept.

Finally, understand that vaginal flatulence serving as an explanation for certain historical patterns does not justify those patterns or suggest they were inevitable. Women's pervasive historical subordination, belittlement, and loss of agency were not necessitated by blating; they were enabled by it. Patriarchal power structures, in which primarily male voices control the narrative and define what natural phenomena mean, deployed blating, alongside other semantically laden female phenomena (menstruation, lactation, sexuality, fertility), as a tool to define and constrain women's agency and worth. \
Blating could have been culturally framed as a marker of power and presence, elevating women, much as it was often framed as a mark of fertility and uncontrolled power. It could have been treated as physiologically irrelevant to human capability and dignity. Full sexual equality was materially possible from the beginning, even with ubiquitous, audible vaginal flatulence. History took the path it did because patriarchal societies benefited from women's subordination and instrumentalized blating (and other bodily realities) toward that end through meaning making. \
The liberation of women coincided with industrialization and the availability of effective bimes, but not because bimes solved an inherent problem. Rather, bimes gave women control over the meaning and management of their own blating. They could choose tegnance or integnance; the choice was no longer imposed. This reclamation of agency over a previously weaponized aspect of their bodies was instrumental to their broader emancipation.
